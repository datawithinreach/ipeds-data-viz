Coverage of Ivy League admissions almost always leads with one number: the admit rate. It&rsquo;s easy to read, easy to compare, and trends in the same direction year after year — down. But the admit rate has stopped being a useful way to tell the eight Ivies apart. Seven of them now admit between 3.6% and 5.4% of applicants. Cornell&rsquo;s 8.8% is the outlier, and even there the absolute number of admits is large because Cornell is large.

The number that still moves is **yield** — the share of admitted students who actually enroll. Yield is harder to game. A school can drive its admit rate down by recruiting more applicants, but it can&rsquo;t drive its yield up without losing cross-admit battles to peer institutions.

## A 20-point gap among schools that admit similar shares

Brown, Penn, and Dartmouth all admitted 5.4% of applicants this cycle — a three-way tie at the third decimal place. Their yields are 65%, 68%, and 69%. Modest spread.

But compare Harvard and Cornell. Harvard admitted 3.6% of applicants and Cornell admitted 8.8% — a 5-point gap on the most-cited number. The yield gap is 20 points: Harvard converts 84% of admits to matriculants, Cornell converts 64%. Harvard is sending out half as many admission letters as Cornell, and a much higher fraction of them stick.

## The funnel tells the truer story

Selectivity is really three numbers stacked: applicants, admitted, enrolled. Looking at all three together makes Cornell&rsquo;s situation specific. Cornell receives the most applications of any Ivy (62,993), admits the most students (5,516), and matriculates the largest first-year class (3,525). It&rsquo;s simultaneously the largest and the least selective Ivy on every step of the funnel.

Harvard&rsquo;s funnel shape is the inverse: big at the top (54,000 applicants) and narrow at the bottom (1,647 enrolled), with very little leakage between admitted and enrolled.

## What yield is actually measuring

Yield is partly a measure of brand strength — how many cross-admits choose your offer over a peer&rsquo;s. It&rsquo;s also a measure of how strongly admissions offices recruit known matchmaking signals: early-decision applicants, recruited athletes, legacy applicants, and similar populations all yield close to 100%. Schools that lean heavily on early-decision can post higher yields without necessarily winning more cross-admit battles.

A more honest read is to look at admit rate and yield side by side. The schools that come out ahead on both — Harvard, MIT (not an Ivy, but in the same conversation, with 4.5% / 86%), and Stanford (3.6% / 82%) — are the ones with the strongest combined position: they admit the smallest share *and* keep the highest fraction of those admits. Everyone else has to pick which number to optimize.

## Methodology

All numbers above come from a single IPEDS file: ADM2023 (final release), which collects the 2023–24 admissions cycle. We use three reported variables — applicants total (`APPLCN`), admissions total (`ADMSSN`), and enrolled total (`ENRLT`) — and derive admit rate (admits ÷ applicants) and yield (enrolled ÷ admits). Schools self-report these counts to NCES; we don&rsquo;t adjust them. The same data is publicly available on the [IPEDS website](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/use-the-data) and updates each fall.
